The Strumming Ladder

115 guitar strumming patterns in 10 levels, ordered so that most of them add or remove a stroke or two from a pattern you already know, each with the songs it is heard in and a click track to play along with.

A note from the author. I am not a guitar teacher. I am a student, and I made these materials for my own practice. I am sharing them in case they are useful to someone else, but please do not take them too seriously: I have no experience of teaching anyone but myself.

The one rule: the hand never stops

Your strumming hand swings like a pendulum, down and up, in time with the count. A pattern is a choice of which swings touch the strings and which pass by in the air.

One bar in each feel. Blue arrows are triplet beats that start on an upstroke; dashed arrows are swings that miss the strings.

The rule in words

Your strumming hand swings like a pendulum, down and up, in time with the count. In quarter-note patterns it goes down on every number and comes back up in between without touching the strings. In eighth-note patterns it goes down on every number and up on every +. In sixteenth-note patterns it goes down on the number and the +, up on the e and the a.

Triplets are the one case where the count does not fix the direction. The count is 1 + a 2 + a, three swings to a beat, and three is odd, so the hand goes down-up-down on beats 1 and 3 and up-down-up on beats 2 and 4. In a shuffle the middle count is left out, and the hand is back to down on the number and up on the a.

Listen to a rung

The click track in words

Press ▶ beside a rung's title to hear it as a looping click track, at the tempo set in the bar at the top (92 to start with). The tempo counts quarter notes. In 6/8 and 12/8 the eighth note keeps its length, so their beats, which are three eighths long, come at two thirds of the number shown (about 61 a minute at 92). Swing and shuffle rungs keep the beat at the number shown. Two bars of stick clicks count you in first: ‘1, 3’ and then ‘1, 2, 3, 4’ in 4/4 (in 6/8, ‘1, 4’ and then all six; in 3/4, ‘1’ and then ‘1, 2, 3’), and the pattern starts on the next bar. Untick ‘count-in’ to start straight away. A bell marks count 1 of every bar. A downstroke is a low, round ‘tock’ and an upstroke a short, bright ‘tick’ two octaves higher; an accent is louder; a muted strum is a short ‘chk’ of noise, a palm mute a dull thud, a staccato strum a clipped click and a bass note a low thump. Missed counts are silent unless you turn on ‘tick every beat’, which ticks softly on a beat with no strum and a little louder on a beat that is played. The sounds are made in the browser; there are no audio files.

How to climb

The four steps in words
  1. Rhythm first, no chord. Learn the strumming hand on its own. Rest the fretting hand lightly across all the strings, without pressing, so every strum gives a dead chk and no notes. Strum the pattern like that while counting out loud. Add a chord only when the hand runs by itself.
  2. Slow metronome. 60 bpm for eighth-note rungs, 50–60 bpm for sixteenth-note rungs. Move up about 10 bpm at a time.
  3. One chord, then two. Loop the pattern on one chord for a minute, then over a change such as G to C. The pattern must not hiccup at the change.
  4. Play it with the record. Put on one of the listed songs and strum along – on dead strings, as in step 1, if you do not know the chords. When you can hold the rung through a whole song, climb to the next one.

Level 2 · Straight eighth notes

About this level

Two slots per beat: '1 + 2 + 3 + 4 +'. Downstrokes land on the numbers, upstrokes on the '+'. The level starts with every slot played and then takes upstrokes away; the all-downstroke and bass-note patterns come after. No upstroke is left hanging over a missing beat yet: that is syncopation, and it starts in level 4.

Level 3 · Compound time and waltz

About this level

A change of count, not of difficulty. Until now every beat split into two; in 6/8 it splits into three, and in 3/4 the bar has three beats. Apart from the last rung, the strumming is even and unsyncopated, so it is no harder than level 2 - it only needs a new way of counting.

Level 4 · Syncopated eighth notes

About this level

Syncopation starts when you skip a downstroke ON a beat: the hand still goes down, but misses the strings. An upstroke with no downstroke after it is the sound of syncopation.

Level 5 · Triplets and shuffle

About this level

Three even strums per beat: count '1 + a 2 + a 3 + a 4 + a'. The rungs start with every count played, first all downstrokes and then alternating, and thin them out a strum or two at a time. Then the middle count of every beat is left out. That gap is the shuffle, or swing: the long-short feel of blues, rock and roll and a great deal of folk. The shuffle rungs thin out the same way. Almost every beat is still played (one two-bar rung stops a beat early), so syncopation waits for the next level.

Level 7 · Muting: left hand and right hand

About this level

Silence and 'chk' sounds are part of the rhythm. The left (fretting) hand mutes by letting go; the right (strumming) hand mutes by touching the strings. Same eighth-note motion as before - only the sound changes.

76Left-hand mute: ring, then choke

↓↑↓↑↓↑↓↑1+2+3+4+

HEAR IT IN

Practice rung – the basis of every percussive strum that follows

Left hand. Play two beats ringing, then release the pressure of the chord without lifting off - fingers stay touching, slightly flattened - and strum two beats of dead 'chk'. No pitch should sound. Squeeze again on beat 1.

77Strum and scratch

↓↑↓↑↓↑↓↑1+2+3+4+

HEAR IT IN

Practice rung – the left-hand mute on every second beat

Left hand. One beat of chord, one beat of scratch. The fretting hand pumps: squeeze, release, squeeze, release. Keep the strumming hand moving evenly; only the fretting hand changes the sound.

78Staccato: strum and cut

↓↓↓↓1+2+3+4+

HEAR IT IN

Practice rung – the same cut is used on the off-beats in the next rung

Staccato means short. Strum, then kill the chord straight away so each strum is a clipped 'chop' with silence after it. With fretted chords, relax the left hand right after the strum (as in rung 76, but after the chord has sounded). With open chords, drop the side of the right hand onto the strings instead.

About the songs

A strumming pattern is a teacher's reading of a recording, not a fixed fact: players vary the strum from bar to bar, sections differ, and lessons often simplify. Some pairings are a teacher's strummed arrangement of a song that has no strummed guitar on the record. The song pairings come from guitar-lesson and tab sites and were not checked against the recordings. Treat each song as a place where the pattern fits and can be heard, then let your ear refine it.
